Unit 1: Evaluation

I did my best to enter Unit 1 with openness. I wanted to try every medium, practice, and process as earnestly as I could (for example, not trying to lean on what I perceive to be my strengths or my usual ways of doing things). This was precisely why I enrolled in Foundation: to experiment and discover, not necessarily as a prerequisite for further studies. Fortunately or unfortunately, I remain almost exactly where I began in one respect: still 50/50 between design and fine art. But while I may not have discovered where I want to go, I have certainly begun to discover something about how I want to get there.

I can trace one main thread through the unit: the tension between my analytical mind and my intuition, my instincts. During Research Week, I approached my work largely intellectually. I was perhaps being a little too clever. When Jonathan asked me, at the end of my brief presentation, how the card made me feel, I had no immediate answer. His question stayed with me. I had been so occupied with historical accuracy and all sorts of tangential questions that I had neglected to ask what the card itself communicated to me.

Since then, I have decisively noticed that my best work arises when there is a certain amount of letting go. But that is perhaps putting it too simply. It wasn’t a matter of abandoning planning. Rather: I needed to plan just enough, research just enough, to build a kind of scaffolding. And then, when I could relax my proverbial grip, something else began to take over. Impulses, instincts, synchronicities, fortuitous accidents filled in the spaces I had left open. As if the work began to develop a will of its own.

It’s a delicate balance: too much planning, and it feels like I suffocate the work. Too little, and perhaps there is nothing for the imagination to grab onto. I find myself thinking now—more seriously than I ever have before—about process itself. How do I create the conditions in which I can lose myself in the work, and let the work take the lead?

Sewing and embroidery during Fashion Design brought me into that flow. So did the physical construction of my diorama, which, once I began making it, seemed almost magically to build itself. (One thing suggested another; the materials appeared to be offering their own ideas…) There is something about working with my hands that quiets my analytical mind. Perhaps this is a nudge to use my sketchbook even more than I usually do. Deliberately and regularly.

But I don’t want to make an enemy of my analytical mind. It has been formed and shaped by years of academic and professional work in computer science and mathematics, and it is one of my greatest strengths. I love discovering structures, correspondences, and systems of meaning. And this is important in contemporary art. And my research throughout Unit 1 genuinely informed my work.

  • Sol LeWitt made me question where an artwork actually resides: in the idea, in the instructions, in their execution? What happens when chance and error enter into the realisation of a supposedly predetermined work? I am not exaggerating when I say that this philosophical—if not metaphysical—question was buzzing in the back of my mind throughout the unit.
  • M/M (Paris) showed me how an entire aesthetic universe can be distilled into a handful of modular symbols, and how those symbols can then be reconstructed into endless new forms. There seems to be an almost infinite number of design lessons in that.
  • Dalí, Do Ho Suh, and Giulio Camillo’s Memory Theatre led me directly to my diorama of the artist’s studio as a representation of the artist’s mind. I have become fascinated by how the imagination materialises itself in the world, only to be shaped in return by its own creations. In my diorama, this took the form of the studio as an extension of the artist’s mind, and the mind itself as a kind of studio, with the boundaries between the artwork and the surrounding world dissolving.
  • Nigel Cooke introduced me to the idea of deliberately getting lost in one’s own work. That is, deliberately setting up obstacles, creating things only to eventually remove them, concealing things in order to later excavate them, thus allowing unforeseen directions to emerge. The final product is almost always, until the end, out of view. Like the shoreline appearing in the distance after a long journey.

There is another kind of analysis, however, which I believe I am particularly good at: the analysis that comes after creation. And, dare I say, related to psychoanalysis. That is, asking myself: Why did I do that? Why did I make that? What drew me to that? What am I trying to say?

I attribute this, at least in large part, to my practice of dreamwork. I have spent a great deal of time asking myself, Why did I dream that last night? And I am increasingly convinced that this is remarkably close to asking, Why did I make that artwork?

My weaknesses, unsurprisingly, are often simply my strengths carried too far. I tend to perhaps overthink and to perhaps attempt too much at once. And, on a more practical level, I could certainly have documented my processes better.

If I were to repeat Unit 1, I would document my process better, and spend more time brainstorming and making associations before committing to a direction. I did do this for most projects, but I could definitely have done it for longer. (How hard is it to set a timer for 15 minutes and just free associate?) And perhaps I would have started the website a little earlier!

What I take into Unit 2 is a desire to consciously and deliberately create my own creative process. I want to experiment with rhythmic and physical practices that loosen the grip of my analytical mind. I want to try something like Cooke’s deliberate obstacles: to create situations in which I cannot see the destination clearly. I am certainly thrilled that the projects will be longer. One of the frustrations of Unit 1 was that, under such tight time constraints, Research, Process, and Evaluation often became three rather unnatural, successive stages. But that is not how the process should be. I want these activities to flow into one another, to continually act upon and transform one another, spiral-like, not in a straight line.
