Research

Nigel Cooke

b. 1973, Manchester, UK
Lives and works in Kent

A British painter whose work blends figurative imagery with abstract, elemental atmospheres. He studied at the Royal College of Art, then completed a PhD in philosophy at Goldsmiths on non-linear systems. He draws from a wide range of subject matter: paleontology, neuroscience, classical mythology, and zoology. His linear compositions suggest brain circuitry, bodies, and landscapes at once. He stains raw linen and builds up layers of paint, and leaves the process of painting visible in the finished work. His work is in the collections of MoMA, the Guggenheim and Tate.

Source: Pace Gallery

Nigel Cooke — New Accursed Art Club (2007)

Nigel Cooke seems to deliberately move himself away from knowing the final destination. He intentionally sets up blinds, dead ends, and obstacles in his work: adding things he knows he will remove, painting the whole thing white and “excavating” the image underneath, liberally erasing. His process involves getting lost. The painting becomes mysterious even to him. The characters in the painting even begin to speak on their own…

Although he doesn’t make collages in the conventional sense, there is something very collage-like about his process: images are assembled, obscured, fragmented, removed, and recombined, often with no clear idea of how the parts will eventually relate to one another. Rather than having a predetermined composition already in his mind, he allows unexpected elements to emerge as well as the relationships between them.

The paintings spawned by New Accursed Art Club (2007):

How does this research inform my own work?

I want to use these ideas in my own work. I have a tendency to overanalyse and perhaps plan too much, which I think sometimes gets in the way of the creative process. I want to flow with the process, let the process lead, deliberately set up obstacles so I don’t know where I’m headed, use mistakes as fodder for new creative directions, paint over things I don’t like, and allow the artwork to take the lead.

Process

(planning, development, outcomes)

“You will be asked to think about how different spaces can be explored through image making. You will explore this through a range of experimental approaches, beginning with collage.”

The white shape is erased from a magazine cut-out: a whale, foreshortened with its tail towards the viewer, that now looks more like a bug or a sea anemone.

I was primarily using images from an interior design magazine. I had this strong interest to collect images where the viewer can’t immediately tell if the image is taken indoors or outdoors: natural things that look a bit artificial (e.g. rectilinear, strongly geometric) and artificial things that look natural (e.g. certain colour palettes, organic forms).

I then thought I was applying PVA glue to stick it all together… apparently it was acrylic medium, which caused the white layer on top. Unfortunately, I didn’t take a picture of the collage prior to the application of the white medium.

I ended up hating the result, so I started again on clean white paper. However, the colours and shapes were largely inspired by the original collage.

The images in the collage shared a very similar palette: neutral tones, set apart only by subtle shifts in hue and temperature. I tried to match those colours closely, and ended up with these gorgeous beige tones: some warm, some cool, some tending towards light green or chartreuse, some leaning almost orange.

I wouldn’t have come up with this palette if I hadn’t been trying to match the collage so closely.

I don’t like it. There is too much going on. However, that was intentional: I lean towards maximalism, and I really wanted to try a bunch of different ideas in one piece. Although I don’t like the result, I can see at least a handful of ideas that I’d like to explore further: pastel on acrylic, painting with a neutral palette where the tones shift ever so slightly, scraping into pastel, and multiple standalone images as one image.

Reflection + Evaluation

My final piece was a mixed-media work: acrylic paint layered with oil pastel and some coloured pencil. I wasn’t happy with it. Somehow the whole thing managed to look both unfinished and overworked. And too much was happening. Oh the blow to the ego! I think I was trying to experiment with too many things in a single painting: an enormous range of colour, several materials—some of which I had only just begun to understand—and several techniques of application. Then again, perhaps it’s not a final piece at all! Very much like a moment in Nigel Cooke’s process, it’s an experiment, a fertile failure, from which several possibilities have sprung.

What I loved most was the original neutral colour palette, which evolved rather accidentally from my attempts to match the source images. I found myself falling in love with shades of—dare I say—beige: beiges with a breath of green, others warmed by reds and yellows, some hovering somewhere near chartreuse, some near lemon yellow. On their own, these quiet colours are scarcely distinguishable from the tone they originated from, yet when they are all brought together, they awaken something in one another! There’s certainly something pleasurable about working with differences so subtle that the eye has to linger to discover them. I also loved the physical act of layering pastel over an undercolour, then scratching through the surface to uncover the colour beneath. I intend to follow these loose threads further, seeing where they lead.
