Unit 1, Week 4

Research

Björk

b. 1965, Reykjavík, Iceland

An Icelandic singer, songwriter and producer who released her first album at the age of eleven, then fronted the Sugarcubes before going solo with Debut (1993). She is regarded as one of the most influential musicians of her era.

Her work keeps returning to the meeting of technology and nature: she uses new tools (custom software, invented instruments, apps) to explore the natural world, from crystals and tectonic plates to viruses and the human body. Yet a human is always at the centre, making sense of these forces and responding to them emotionally. She works closely with designers, scientists and technologists, treating each album as a whole world of sound, image, object and performance.

Sources: Britannica; Wikipedia

Biophilia

Björk’s Biophilia (2011) was released as the first “app album”: each song having its own app exhibiting the connection between a part of nature and an idea from music theory. Björk turned a large body of research—including research into DNA, into musical form—producing an album that takes on a vast array of subject matter: the rhythms of the human body, the movement of tectonic plates, the workings of the natural universe.

What holds the songs together is a concern with boundaries and how they dissolve: between creative disciplines, between the algorithmic language of code and artistic expression, and between the wonder of nature and the logic of science.

For the album and its tour, she commissioned a set of new instruments. Each was built because no existing instrument could make the sound a song needed, and many play the natural force the song is about: pendulums swung by gravity for “Solstice”, real electrical bolts for “Thunderbolt”.

Gravity harps

Andy Cavatorta, 2011

Björk wanted an instrument that used gravity to make music. The final instrument has three-metre pendulums, each carrying a rotating, 11-string cylindrical harp. One string is plucked every time a pendulum swings through its lowest point: gravity keeps the rhythm, and a computer chooses the notes. Heard on “Solstice”.

Sources: Make; MIT Arts; Sound On Sound

Sharpsichord

Henry Dagg, 2006–10

A 46-note, solar-powered pin-barrel harp programmed like a music box: pins are screwed into a rotating cylinder of 11,520 holes, each pin plucks a string, and two horns amplify the bass and treble separately. Heard on “Sacrifice”.

Sources: Clash; 15 Questions; VICE

Gameleste

Matt Nolan and Björgvin Tómasson, 2011

A vintage orchestral celesta whose steel bars were replaced with bronze ones, then rebuilt and fitted with MIDI. Heard on “Crystalline” and “Virus”.

Sources: AQNB; Sound On Sound; Wikipedia

Tesla coil

A coil that throws bolts of electricity at whatever pitch is fed into it, played from a keyboard. It plays the bass line of “Thunderbolt”; Björk’s musical director called it “by far the loudest thing on stage”.

Sources: Sound On Sound; Wikipedia

Each of these instruments was designed, first of all, to make sound. Its shape is dictated by acoustics, the physics of sound. There is very little room for the maker to deviate. And yet these instruments are also beautiful objects. Henry Dagg (creator of the Sharpsichord) describes his aim as “to make form follow function, with as much visual interest and elegance as possible”, echoing the architect Louis Sullivan’s “form ever follows function, and this is the law” (1896). Frank Lloyd Wright later went further, arguing that form and function should not merely follow one another but “be one, joined in a spiritual union.”

If an instrument’s form is almost entirely fixed by the sound it must produce, why should it also be beautiful? Why should something designed for the ear be so pleasing to the eye? It seems like too much of a coincidence. Perhaps it suggests that beauty is not simply in the eye of the beholder, but something objective, something recognisable by all of our senses?

References: Henry Dagg, interview with 15 Questions, 2022; Louis Sullivan, “The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered”, Lippincott’s Magazine, March 1896 (see Wikipedia); Frank Lloyd Wright, 1957 recording (see Open Culture).

The Follyphone

Lewis Sydney — The Follyphone

b. c. 1872 (sources differ)
d. 1941

Lewis Sydney was a British actor, comedian, mimic, and pianist. He was known for impersonating musical instruments in sketches, such as the tin whistle in Tchaikovsky’s 1812, and later had a long career as a society entertainer at the piano.

He invented the Follyphone—a deliberately absurd musical instrument, combining an organ-like keyboard with an assortment of pipes, horns, levers, and stops. Despite its elaborate construction and imposing appearance, the instrument often produced little more than silence or a few feeble notes.

Sources: Pélissier’s Follies; IMSLP; Paleofuture

The Biophilia instruments were built to make sounds, and they are interesting to look at as a product of that function. The Follyphone is sort of the opposite: it barely functions as an instrument at all. And yet we recognise it immediately as a musical instrument. If its form isn’t dictated by sound, what are we recognising?

We are recognising some sort of a visual vocabulary that we’ve learned. The Follyphone strings these signs together into a kind of sentence, grammatically correct but with nothing to say. It follows the visual rules of an instrument without following the physical principles that gave rise to those rules in the first place.

How does this research inform my own work?

For me, this assignment and the research around it are interesting primarily on a conceptual level. Musical instruments are a great focal point for the debate between form and function: their shapes are dictated almost entirely by the sounds they must make, yet so many are beautiful objects in their own right. They are also a rich subject for dissecting the visual vocabulary and grammar of objects: the Follyphone barely works, yet we read it at once as an instrument, because it speaks a visual language we have learned from instruments that do.

Reflection + Evaluation
  • This project made me realise that designing assignments for art students is an art in and of itself. It’s a brilliant brief: it encourages everyone to draw on a shared visual vocabulary—what makes something recognisable as a musical instrument?—while leaving room for individual interpretation.
  • It also made me think more seriously about the nature of materials. We were “just” working with cardboard, but, as it turns out, there are many different kinds: some bend more easily, others are better suited to rigid construction, and even their colours and textures vary enormously. Within what seemed like a very simple restriction, I discovered a surprising amount of depth. I imagine this is true of almost any material. You really have to spend time playing with it to understand its properties and limitations—what it does, what it resists, and what you can coax out of it.
  • A smaller observation, but one that made me laugh to myself: I have quite a few architect friends, and they’ve told me there’s a good reason you don’t see too many curves in buildings out in the wild. Curves are difficult to work with, and they posed quite a structural challenge for me too. A simple thing, intellectually speaking, but I came to understand it through my hands, which is a different thing entirely. When you’re constructing something, rectilinearity makes life easy; anything that departs from it demands more thought.
