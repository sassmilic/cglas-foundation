Quick Facts

  • 10% of global carbon emissions come from fashion production.
  • 85% of textiles are discarded each year.
  • Fashion is the second-largest industrial consumer of water.
  • Synthetic clothing releases huge quantities of microplastics.
  • Textile dyeing is a major source of water pollution.

Chloe Baines

Lives and works in London, UK

A fashion designer working almost entirely with waste materials, Baines began using recycled materials on an art and design foundation course in Coventry, then studied at London College of Fashion, where a seminar by Christopher Raeburn on remaking discarded parachutes shaped her approach.

Her collection In Tents: The After Party (2019) grew out of the 2018 Boomtown festival, where so many tents were abandoned that “it looked as though nobody else had actually left”. She collected the damaged and unwanted tents and remade them into tops, dresses and skirts.

Sources: Dezeen; Bricks Magazine

How does this research inform my own work?

Baines works with the material rather than against it, allowing its existing character to remain legible. I’m inspired by this honest approach to material: rather than disguising its past, she lets this previous life stay visible.

Process

(planning, development, outcomes)

I wanted to take a large, boxy men’s T-shirt and turn it into a fitted women’s dress, not by adding anything but by cutting shapes away: a diagonal from the front, triangles from the arms.

I found this to be interesting technically because a man’s T-shirt has almost no shaping. It is basically rectangles cut to hang straight off the shoulders. Fit normally comes from darts and fabric cut to follow the curves of the body. Here it has to come from subtraction.

I wanted to cover the graffiti-like lettering of the screen-printed Nike logo on the back with embroidery, hiding “Nike” completely under stitches inspired by its shapes. As I worked, the loops and hollows of the letters started turning into something like a pond. The piece is still unfinished, but I would love to add water lilies, flowers and other details to push the pond idea further.

I covered the other Nike logo, the embroidered patch on the front, using techniques from darning.

I didn’t want to bother anyone with helping me on the photoshoot, so I decided to do it myself.

Didn’t turn out great, but it’s done 👍

Reflection + Evaluation

As much as I enjoy buying and wearing clothes, I have something of a psychological complex about fashion as a creative discipline. I see clothing—at least, the kind that interests me—largely as craftsmanship: I want the things I buy to be well made, from good materials, and any quirkiness or uniqueness is a bonus. Throughout this project, a voice in my head kept insisting that no one would ever wear what I was making, that it was all simply too ridiculous. I think this placed a roadblock in front of my creativity. Safe to say that fashion design is probably not for me.

But something wonderful came out of it: I became completely absorbed in embroidery, and have continued working with it since. I’ve even begun to envision, in some detail, how I might use it in my final project. I’d felt myself becoming a little creatively blocked after the first few weeks of the programme, and I realised that the repetitive, rhythmic act of stitching put me into a kind of flow state, quietening my analytical mind. As my hands worked, ideas seemed to float up from my subconscious. Really good ideas just kept bubbling up, almost without effort.

This made me begin to reflect on what my artistic process will grow to become. During Research Week, I wrote about how I felt like my analytical side was getting in the way of my creativity and experimentation. I now realise that something rhythmic and hands-on—embroidery, in particular—can be a wonderful way of loosening its grip. Interestingly, there is even some neuroscience to support the connection between needlework and changes in brain activity.1

1 Davide Rossi Sebastiano et al., “Crochet increases attention through a requiring motor skill learning”, Scientific Reports 15, 4141 (2025).
