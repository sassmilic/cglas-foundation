Saša Milić

3D Design — Diorama

Unit 1, Week 5

Research

My point of departure was an interest of mine that had been developing prior to this project: the relationship between our inner/psychic space and the physical spaces we inhabit. On one hand, our surroundings can profoundly influence our mental and emotional states—consider the deliberate architecture and atmosphere of temples and ritual spaces. Conversely, we project our inner worlds onto our surroundings, shaping them to reflect our thoughts, emotions, and identities. This is certainly touching on some architectural interests.

Salvador Dalí

b. 1904, Figueres, Spain
d. 1989, Figueres, Spain

A leading figure of Surrealism, Dalí was known for his meticulously rendered, dreamlike imagery and exploration of the unconscious mind. Influenced by Freudian psychoanalysis, he developed his paranoiac-critical method to transform irrational associations and perceptions into art. His practice extended beyond painting into sculpture, film, and design. He collaborated with Luis Buñuel on Un Chien Andalou (1929), a landmark of Surrealist cinema. His experiments with sculptural objects, installations, and architecture extended his exploration of the unconscious into physical, three-dimensional space.

Sources: National Gallery of Art; Fundació Gala–Salvador Dalí; MoMA

MoMA suggests that the piece was an attempt to reconstruct a mysterious optical theatre Dalí had encountered as a child. Dalí was apparently attempting to reconstruct a remembered experience of illusion, rather than simply reproducing an object or scene.

The Little Theatre blurs the boundary between the real and the imagined. We instinctively try to construct a story from its strange collection of elements, but the composition resists a coherent narrative. Nevertheless, there is a palpable relationship between them, an internal logic that we sense but cannot quite articulate—much like a dream, where everything seems connected like a tenuous fibre even when nothing quite makes sense.

How does this research inform my own work?

Dalí has said, “My best ideas com[e] through my dreams.” I am inspired by Dalí’s confidence to take inspiration directly from his dreams and completely ignore the rational mind. Dreams are so personal and often impenetrable that we perhaps may resist going directly to them for inspiration. I think it’s also very clear that this all is very much related to my interest in psychic/headspace and how one may materialise that in form.

Do Ho Suh

b. 1962, Seoul, South Korea
Lives and works in London, UK

A South Korean contemporary artist working across sculpture, installation, drawing, and film, Suh explores the relationship between physical space, memory, identity, and the concept of home. He is best known for his life-size architectural replicas of spaces he has inhabited, meticulously constructed from translucent, coloured fabric. These delicate, seemingly weightless structures blur the boundaries between physical reality and memory, between the interior psyche and exterior architecture. His work often features transitional spaces—corridors, staircases, and doorways—reflecting his interest in migration, belonging, and the fluid relationship between people and the spaces they inhabit.

Sources: Victoria Miro; Lehmann Maupin

How does this research inform my own work?

Suh’s work encourages the viewer to think of architectural space as something more than a physical structure: a vessel of memories and emotions. His use of translucent fabrics is particularly interesting. It suggests the permeability between physical and psychic space. I would like to explore how specific materials may be better at communicating this relationship.

Giulio Camillo (Delminio)

b. c. 1480, Friuli, Italy
d. 1544, Milan, Italy

An Italian Renaissance philosopher, humanist, and scholar of rhetoric, Camillo was deeply influenced by Neoplatonism, Hermeticism, and Kabbalah. He is best known for his Memory Theatre, an extraordinary attempt to organise all human knowledge within a single symbolic architectural structure. His vision united the classical art of memory (ars memoriae)1 with Renaissance esotericism, treating the architecture of the mind as a reflection of the cosmic order.

1 The classical art of memory (ars memoriae) was a mnemonic technique developed in ancient Greece and Rome, particularly for memorising speeches. Using the method of loci (“places”), practitioners mentally placed vivid images representing information within familiar architectural spaces, then recalled them by imaginatively walking through those spaces.

Sources: Treccani, Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani; Lina Bolzoni, Cabinet

How does this research inform my own work?

What interests me here is the attempt to give physical form to something fundamentally immaterial: the contents of the mind. The theatre was simultaneously a mnemonic device, an architectural model of consciousness, and a symbolic representation of the cosmos. It suggests that architecture might do more than influence our psyches and internal experiences; it might actually externalise the structures of the mind itself.

Process

At first I was dancing around the idea of ritual spaces, even going so far as imagining a Freemasons’ building. Then, somewhere along the way, I landed on the artist’s studio, because this too is a kind of temple or ritual space, even an initiatory one. I started playing with that idea: the studio as an externalisation of the artist’s mind, and the artwork as something that extends beyond the physical space, bringing the cosmos onto a canvas. It was hard to pin down anything concrete, but these were the ideas I was circling, and I felt they were enough to begin work.

I wanted a checkered floor. The glue made the paper wet and it started to lift and ripple. I’ll come up with a solution, or a way to use the effect later.

I waited for the PVA glue to dry, then used sandpaper to flatten the raised surface, and it started to look like a marble floor!

I wanted at least one or two things in the diorama to be almost hyperrealistic, albeit miniature. The rest of the scene would be rather abstract, so the realistic elements ground the piece as a whole: they give the viewer a point of reference, an anchor, so they know what they’re looking at.

This is the painting, the artist’s mind coming to life. The folded paper structures are inspired by apartment blocks, but they are intentionally not fully realised or painted in detail.

The viewer should resolve the structure in their own way.

The colours of the paints largely mirror the colours on the floor of the abstract part of the diorama, which represents the artist’s mind and the painting coming to life.

I found this little piece of wood in the box of wood. It was the only unrefined piece, and I was inspired by its organic forms: I felt my diorama was missing just that. I picked some leaves outside and attached them to the wood to make a mini tree.

I joined the pieces with bendy metal wire, because it felt more in line with the focus on materiality and tactility.

Reflection + Evaluation

I loved doing this project, certainly one of my top three from Unit 1. I think it was because everything about it felt organic: the ideas flowed one after another, and synchronicities emerged to help me along the way. The whole process was so meta—creating an artwork of an artist’s studio and an artist’s mind as their work comes to life—that I was intoxicated by it all.

Once I began constructing, I fell into a real flow, and things just started happening. The process of making the diorama started, weirdly, to mirror what I was trying to express through it. I had begun with research into the relationship between physical and psychic space, how others have expressed the architecture of the mind and the materialisation of the imagination… And then, when it came to actually making something, I found myself working almost entirely intuitively, with ideas almost magically materialising before me.

Things seemed to suggest themselves to me. I thought the glue had ruined my checkered floor, but sanding it produced something resembling marble. I came across a peculiar little piece of wood, and it became a tree. The colours of the leaves I picked outside happened to match the already-painted green and yellow portions of the floor. That tree then became the clear subject of the painting within the diorama, another world within the world I was constructing.

It was as though the diorama had become a mirror of its own making: a miniature world about the creative mind, assembled through precisely the mysterious workings of mine.

After reflecting so much in previous projects on how my analytical mind can interfere with my creativity, it was enormously satisfying to experience the opposite. I had done the research, I had thought about the ideas, but at a certain point I could simply let go and allow the work to magically develop its own internal logic.
