Rembrandt — etchings

Rembrandt, Self-portrait in a cap, with eyes wide open, 1630, etching and burin. Public domain image.

Rembrandt Harmenszoon van Rijn (born 15 July 1606 in Leiden, Dutch Republic) has an estimated 300 etchings in his surviving work.

Early in his career, he used a style based on drawing but soon moved to one based on painting, using a mass of lines and different strengths of line. Towards the end of the 1630s, he reacted against this manner and moved to a simpler style.

The Adoration of the Shepherds: Nocturne, Rembrandt van Rijn (1606–1669), etching and drypoint, first state, c. 1657.

Details of line work from Rembrandt’s etchings, colours inverted to make the lines clearer.

His line work is so expressive. There is such a variety/scale, from dense tangles of lines to completely untouched space.

Note to self: really play and exaggerate and focus on the line work when doing etchings. Line work is everything here.

Detail of a Rembrandt etched portrait, 1636, with surface tone over much of the hat.

Goya — Los Caprichos

Details from Goya’s Los Caprichos, etchings and aquatints.

Goya owned prints by Rembrandt, and the two are often compared. Paul-André Lemoisne points out the difference: Rembrandt worked like a painter-engraver, often sketching straight onto the plate, whereas Goya first drew the subject meticulously on paper, then simplified and lightened it when he etched it.

Goya’s linework is much neater. There’s a certain freedom and complete confidence about Rembrandt that I prefer.

Goya, Los caprichos (Caprices), plate 40, ¿De que mal morira? (What Illness Will He Die from?), original etching and aquatint, 1796–97. Fifth edition, late nineteenth century, 19 × 14 cm. Image: National Library of Medicine.
